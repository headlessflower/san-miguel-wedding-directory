# What Weather to Expect for a San Miguel de Allende Wedding

The first thing Elena did after getting engaged was not make a guest list. It was not choose colors. It was not even send me venues, though she had already saved enough courtyards and rooftops to make her phone feel warm.

She opened the weather app.

This is a very normal thing to do, and also a mostly useless thing to do when your wedding is fourteen months away. Weather apps are built for people deciding whether to carry an umbrella tomorrow. They are less helpful for a person trying to imagine whether their grandmother will be cold during cocktail hour next February, or whether the string lights will still look romantic if the sky opens at 6:40 p.m. in July.

That was Elena's problem. She was a planner by temperament, but not in the glossy, clipboard way. She liked knowing how things worked. She wanted to understand the city before asking eighty people to fly there. San Miguel de Allende looked beautiful in every photograph, which was part of the difficulty. A rooftop at sunset in March, a garden after rain in August, and a candlelit courtyard in November can all look like the correct answer.

But weather is not just scenery. It changes the pace of the day. It decides when guests arrive, what they wear, where the ceremony happens, whether the dance floor stays outside, and how much faith you should put in an uncovered dinner table.

San Miguel is generous, but it is not neutral. It has opinions.

## The city has altitude, dry air, and real seasons

San Miguel sits high in central Mexico, roughly 6,300 feet above sea level. You feel that in small ways before you know how to name it. The sun can feel direct at noon. The shade can feel suddenly cool. A day that starts with a sweater can turn into a lunch where everyone is searching for the one seat under the umbrella.

This is one of the first weather lessons for a wedding here: do not plan only for the average temperature. Plan for the movement of the day.

According to long-term climate averages, San Miguel's daily highs often range from the low 70s in the coolest months to the mid 80s in late spring. Nights can drop much lower, especially in winter. Rain is highly seasonal. The wetter stretch is generally early June through late September, with July usually bringing the most rain. The driest months sit closer to November through April, with December especially dry.

That sounds straightforward. Dry season good, rainy season complicated. Done.

Except it is not quite that simple.

Some of the most beautiful San Miguel days happen near the edges, when the city is changing from one mood to another. April has light that makes walls look awake. October can feel freshly washed. August can turn a garden almost electric after an afternoon storm. The question is not really "Which month is perfect?" The question is, "What kind of uncertainty can I live with, and what kind of beauty am I hoping for?"

Elena did not like that answer at first. Most people do not. We prefer months to behave like products with clean feature lists.

Weather does not do that. How fun. Not really.

## December through February: clear days, cool nights, and guests who need layers

Winter in San Miguel is often the easiest season to romanticize. The days can be clear and comfortable, and the city has that dry, crisp quality that makes walking feel like the correct form of transportation. You can wander from a hotel courtyard to the Jardin, hear church bells, buy coffee, and briefly convince yourself that all logistics are solvable if the light is nice enough.

For weddings, winter is appealing because rain risk is low. December is usually one of the driest months of the year. January is typically the coldest month at night, with average lows in the low 40s Fahrenheit, even while daytime highs can still reach the low 70s.

That split matters. A noon scouting walk and an 8 p.m. outdoor dinner are not the same climate.

If Elena chose January, the ceremony could probably happen outside. Cocktail hour could be beautiful. Dinner could work too, but not casually. She would need heaters, shawls, a note on the wedding website, and maybe a venue layout that lets older guests drift inside without feeling like they have left the party.

Winter is good for:

- Couples who want lower rain risk
- Guests who like walking and sightseeing during the day
- Courtyard ceremonies, rooftop cocktails, and tent-free daytime events
- Formal dress codes that make more sense when the evening cools down

Winter asks for:

- Heaters or pashminas for outdoor dinners
- A guest note about cool evenings
- Careful timing if the ceremony starts late
- A venue with an indoor or protected area that still feels intentional

The mistake would be assuming Mexico means warm at every hour. San Miguel is not a beach. It is a highland city, and winter nights can remind you of that without apologizing.

## March through May: bright, dry, warmer, and more exposed

By March, San Miguel starts to feel more open. The air is still mostly dry, and the sky can be generous. April and May bring warmer days, with May often the hottest month on average. WeatherSpark lists May around an average high of 84 degrees Fahrenheit and an average low around 56.

For a wedding, this is tempting. The rain risk is still much lower than summer, flowers can look beautiful, and the evenings are less likely to feel cold than January. This is the season where photos can trick you into thinking the city was built entirely for golden hour.

But spring has its own pressure. The sun is stronger. The landscape can feel dry before the rains arrive. A ceremony in full afternoon exposure may look lovely in the floor plan and feel punishing in person. Guests will not remember the exact floral palette if they spent twenty-five minutes quietly wondering whether their chair was getting hotter.

This was where Elena had to admit something about herself. She wanted the whole weekend to feel effortless, but she also wanted control. Spring gave her control over rain, mostly. It did not give her control over heat or shade.

So the planning question changed.

Not: "Will it rain?"

More like: "Where is the sun at 4:30 p.m., and where will my guests be standing?"

Spring is good for:

- Outdoor ceremonies with strong shade planning
- Rooftop welcome parties
- Couples who want a drier season without the coolest winter nights
- Wedding weekends with walking tours, lunches, and daytime gatherings

Spring asks for:

- Later ceremony times when possible
- Shade, fans, hydration, and shorter exposed transitions
- A realistic look at ceremony orientation
- Sunscreen and water available without making it feel like a school field trip

March and April can feel like a very clean answer. May is still beautiful, but it is more direct. It is the month that says, yes, the photos will be good, but please respect the sun.

## June through September: rain season, greener gardens, and the need for a real Plan B

The rainy season is the part people either fear too much or respect too little.

Long-term averages show the wetter season running roughly from early June through late September, with July usually bringing the highest rainfall and the most wet days. The rains do not necessarily mean every day is ruined. Often, the pattern is more dramatic than constant: clearer morning, building clouds, afternoon or evening rain, then that washed-stone smell that makes the city feel newly made.

There is a version of San Miguel in summer that is hard to get in the dry months. The hills soften. Gardens look alive. The sky becomes interesting instead of merely blue. A storm moving behind the Parroquia can make everyone stop talking for a second, which is not a bad thing for a wedding weekend, unless dinner was supposed to be under that exact piece of sky.

This is the season for couples who can hold two ideas at once.

One: the city may be beautiful.

Two: the rain plan is not optional.

Elena had a friend who loved the idea of an August wedding because the city would be greener and flights might be easier. That friend was not wrong. But an August wedding cannot be designed around denial. The covered option needs to be as considered as the first option. Not a hallway. Not a sad backup room with stackable chairs and emergency lighting. A real second version of the wedding.

Rainy season is good for:

- Couples who love lush gardens and dramatic skies
- Venues with beautiful covered terraces, arcades, tents, or indoor rooms
- More flexible timelines
- Smaller weddings that can move more easily if weather shifts

Rainy season asks for:

- A rain plan you genuinely like
- Covered paths between ceremony, cocktails, dinner, and bathrooms
- Vendor timelines that include weather contingencies
- Transportation planning, because rain changes walking in cobblestone streets
- Flexible photography plans for both sun and storm light

If you choose June through September, do not think of Plan B as an emergency. Think of it as the second draft of the same story. It should still sound like you.

## October and November: the useful middle ground

October is where many couples start to relax. The heaviest rain risk usually begins to fade by late September, and the city can still carry some of the green from summer. November moves more fully into the dry season, with comfortable days and cooler evenings.

This is the season I think Elena wanted before she knew how to say it. Not the absolute driest month. Not the warmest month. Not the greenest month. Something steadier.

October can still ask for rain awareness, especially early in the month, but it usually feels less like you are negotiating with the sky every afternoon. November feels more settled. Days are often pleasant. Nights cool down enough that guests may want layers, but not usually with the same sharpness as January.

For destination weddings, this middle ground matters because guests are not only attending the ceremony. They are arriving, wandering, eating late, recovering the next morning, and trying to understand the city through their bodies. Weather shapes all of that. A comfortable November afternoon can make the whole weekend feel more generous.

October and November are good for:

- Couples who want outdoor events with lower risk than summer
- Garden or hacienda weddings
- Multi-day guest itineraries
- A balance between dry-season comfort and post-rain-season color

October and November ask for:

- A light rain plan in October
- Layers or heaters for later November nights
- Earlier sunset planning as the year moves on
- Clear guest guidance for uneven temperatures between day and night

This is not a dramatic recommendation, but it is probably the most practical one: if you want an outdoor San Miguel wedding and do not want weather to become the main character, start by looking at October, November, March, and April. Then adjust based on your tolerance for cold nights, hot afternoons, and rain plans.

## A month-by-month way to think about it

Here is the practical version Elena eventually wrote in her notes. It was less elegant than the Pinterest board, but more useful.

| Month | What to expect | Wedding planning note |
|---|---|---|
| January | Dry, clear, coolest nights | Great for low rain risk; plan heaters and guest layers |
| February | Dry, mild days, cool evenings | Strong outdoor option with evening comfort planning |
| March | Warmer, dry, bright | Good for outdoor events; watch sun exposure |
| April | Warm, mostly dry, high sunlight | Beautiful, but shade and timing matter |
| May | Hottest average month, rain beginning to rise | Later ceremonies, hydration, shade, and flexibility |
| June | Rain season begins in earnest | Covered plans become important |
| July | Wettest month on average | Only choose venues with a rain plan you like |
| August | Green, cloudy, still rainy | Good for lush settings; build the day around contingencies |
| September | Cloudier and still wet | Dramatic skies, but logistics matter |
| October | Rain risk easing, greener than spring | Often a strong compromise month |
| November | Drier, pleasant days, cooler nights | Excellent outdoor potential with light evening layers |
| December | Very dry, cooler evenings | Beautiful for celebrations; plan warmth after sunset |

## What guests need to know

Guests do not need a meteorological essay. They need a few honest sentences.

For winter: "Days are usually mild, but evenings can be cool. Please bring a jacket or wrap."

For spring: "Expect warm, sunny days. Some events may be outdoors, so sunglasses and comfortable shoes are helpful."

For rainy season: "This is San Miguel's greener season, and afternoon rain is possible. We have covered spaces planned, and cobblestone-friendly shoes are recommended."

For October or November: "Days are generally comfortable, with cooler evenings. Layers are a good idea."

That kind of language does two things. It helps people pack, and it quietly tells them the couple has thought about their comfort. That matters. Hospitality is not always a welcome bag. Sometimes it is telling someone they will be cold before they discover it personally.

## The real decision

After a while, Elena stopped asking which month was best. She started asking which version of the wedding felt honest.

If she wanted the cleanest outdoor odds, she would choose the dry season and accept cooler evenings or warmer spring sun. If she wanted green gardens and dramatic clouds, she would choose summer and pay serious attention to covered spaces. If she wanted balance, she would look hard at October or November.

That is probably the best way to plan weather in San Miguel. Not by pretending the city will behave, but by listening to what each season is already telling you.

A good wedding plan does not remove weather from the day. It makes room for it.

And in San Miguel, that might mean heaters glowing in a January courtyard, fans turning under an April terrace, rain tapping above a covered dinner in July, or guests walking home in November with jackets over their shoulders and the stones still holding a little warmth from the afternoon.

That is not a perfect forecast.

It is better. It is a plan.
